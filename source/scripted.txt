Action, authorisation and account across nine connected domains, with three ways to position them. Every function in society answers three different questions: what is being done, whether it may be done, and what account is kept of it. The Nine Bodies of Authority gives each question its own position and repeats that pattern across nine domains, from government to everyday

cultural life. The model classifies functions, not institutions. One organisation can hold several positions, and one person can move between them in a single day, but the positions never merge. The model has two parts. Parts I to V set out the nine bodies: the three positions, the principle that keeps them apart, the nine domains, and the loop that

connects them, demonstrated through two worked cases. Part VI places the bodies on a three-bit cube that records how open each body is to the person in front of it, and sets out three routes through that cube. Part VII records what is settled, how the model was built, and its terms. An embodied agent enacts or requests. An authoritative

body approves or denies. An authoritative agent records and reviews. The returned account decides what happens next. An action shows that something happened. An approval shows that a designated body allowed it. A reviewed account shows the conditions, the process and the consequences. None of the three stands in for another. An approved action can still be carried out badly,

and a well-executed action can still have been wrongly approved. This is what makes responsibility locatable. When an operation fails, the model separates four failures: an inadequate proposal, an unjustified approval, poor execution, and an incomplete or misleading account. They can occur together, but each one demands its own remedy. The three positions form a circuit, and the circuit has

two movements. The first is submission. The embodied agent enacts and submits: it carries something into effect and puts it forward for decision. Enact is chosen over act deliberately. To act is to perform; to enact is to give something formal effect, as a law is enacted or an authorised order carried out. The authoritative body approves or denies. The

authoritative agent records and reviews that decision and returns the account to the embodied agent. The second is the continuing loop. Holding the returned account, the embodied agent now responds and requests: it answers what came back and asks for what comes next. The authoritative body approves or denies again, the authoritative agent records and reviews again, and the account

comes back again. The account belongs to the agent who receives it, to use, to influence and to decide upon. The third position is separate from the authoritative body, yet belongs to it: it is the body’s self-examination. It never approves or denies, and it never acts. It consolidates an account of what the body has done, reviews that account

against the conditions around it and against baseline comparisons, and returns a recorded assessment that informs the next request and the next decision. The embodied agent is a position, not a headcount: a whole workforce can occupy it. The account does not wait for the action to finish. Existing records inform a request before it is made, an approval while

it is considered, and an intervention while it runs. A body of authority is the whole triad, not just its approving position. The model begins with concentrated power. A body that can watch people, interpret what it sees and act against them, all under one roof, answers to no one. Picture a county police force. Its officers patrol and respond

in person, among the people they serve. Now give the same office ten thousand live camera feeds from the suburb it patrols, an analytic system to read them, and the authority to dispatch on whatever that system flags. The officers no longer meet the public on equal terms. They watch it from a room. That is the arrangement the model

refuses. The three capabilities are therefore held apart. Surveillance keeps the record, intelligence weighs information and authorises operations, and enforcement carries them out. They cooperate case by case, under authorisation that is itself recorded and reviewable. Every handoff runs through a defined channel: who may request footage, for what purpose, who approves the request, what analysis is permitted, what is

logged, and who may challenge it. The live monitoring of a whole population never sits in the hands of the people who make arrests. Cooperation never grants access. Taking part in a shared process gives no position the infrastructure, resources or powers of another. Every triad is built by the same test: which capabilities must never be held together? Each

position is defined as much by what it cannot do as by what it does. The three positions are distinct and cooperating. None is subordinate to another and none dominates. No triad dominates another. Within each triad the authoritative body decides whether an action proceeds, which is power over that action and never power over the other bodies. Intelligence decides

operations in body 3 exactly as the judicial body decides applications in body 2. Intelligence shows why the rule matters most where power gathers most. It is the apex of body 3, the point where information is fed up to the directors and heads who decide. Because it decides, it holds neither the cameras nor the hands. Surveillance keeps the

record, enforcement keeps the field, intelligence keeps the decision, and each weighs against the other two like the arms of a balance. Auditorial is the one term the model coins. It names a power every person holds simply by existing: the power to scrutinise institutions, individually and collectively, without holding any of their other powers. Through it the public, as

its own collective, watches everything else. The auditorial position records and reviews authorised material, procedures and outcomes. It cannot argue, judge, enforce, confine or surveil. However strong its findings, it never turns them into a ruling or an arrest by its own hand. Cross-examination here means testing accounts against one another inside the report, not advocacy in a courtroom. The report

is a bounded assessment others act upon, and its findings stay open to challenge. Auditorial has no hands, so its findings travel through the chain that already exists. Whether a flagged matter is acted on rests on precedent, set by the judicial body in earlier cases. Precedent rests on legislation and on the legal argument that interprets it. Both rest

on the constitutional enactment of government, which rests on what the public wants, and the whole national order sits within international law and the diplomatic relations that carry it. A finding enters that chain as a concern. The chain decides the outcome, and any order or enforcement that follows belongs to the body authorised to give it. That limit is

what bounds its capacity for corruption. A position that cannot argue, judge, enforce, confine or surveil cannot convert what it finds into power. Legal and judicial power shape how auditorial scrutiny is conducted, and auditorial scrutiny holds legal and judicial power to account. Auditorial inspects how power was used. It does not watch private life. The line runs between evidence

of institutional conduct, which it examines, and the private lives of people, which it never enters. Holding that line keeps public scrutiny from becoming universal surveillance. Classification follows the activity being performed, not the organisation performing it. A bank deciding whether to extend a loan occupies the commercial position. The same bank maintaining and reviewing the account of balances, obligations

and repayments occupies the financial position. The two happen in one building, often at one desk, and they remain two functions. A journalist who makes entertainment is creating content at that moment, and a creator who investigates a public claim is doing journalism at that moment. The three positions repeat across nine domains. Each domain is a complete body of

authority. Every name is held to one or two words, so the names stay comparable across the table. Governmental agents turn public concern into proposals and administer what is approved, including military defence. The constitutional body tests every proposal against the foundational distribution of powers and protections, in the manner of a supreme court. Its permission is not endorsement: a

constitutional approval says a proposal is lawful, not that it is wise. A constitution is something a government must adhere to and something a people can amend, and amendment is a process of its own: public agreement does not rewrite a constitution automatically. Diplomatic agents hold the account of relationships, commitments and conditions beyond the border. They are the nation’s

representatives and delegates, and each carries the whole authority in a single person. That is what gives the position its review. The diplomat consolidates an account of the nation represented, compares it against the laws, decisions and circumstances of other nations and against the external limits, pressures and baseline comparisons the nation operates within, and returns that assessment home. The

review faces outward in order to inform the inside. Legal agents build arguments, file applications and answer decisions. The judicial body rules on them, and its rulings set the precedent later cases rely on. Auditorial records the evidence, procedure, reasoning and outcome, and returns that account to everyone with standing to question it. Enforcement requests operations and carries them out

in the world. Its agents are the public face of the triad: the officers a person calls, meets and reports to. Intelligence assesses information and decides whether each operation proceeds. It is the knowledge architecture of the triad: the agencies, analysts and systems in which information is gathered, weighed and held, and the heads who decide on it. It is

where this triad’s decisions are made, never its eyes or its hands. Surveillance holds the observational record on infrastructure controlled separately from both. Development logistics covers architecture, design, planning, construction, maintenance and the movement of people and goods. Transport is one expression of it, and its wider function is organising change in the material world. Planning proposals belong here, with

the agents who draw and submit them. Civic welfare approves or denies proposals against community requirements such as access, disruption and the needs of affected residents, and against the health of the population the work touches. Setting district rules is an authorising act and sits here. Property stewardship holds the account of land, buildings, tenure, district and location records, possessions,

ownership claims, use rights and asset condition. It records who owns, claims, occupies or manages what, and checks compliance with the rules civic welfare sets. Coordinating and recording maintenance belongs here; carrying out the repair belongs to development logistics. Maintaining a building and maintaining the record of who owns it are separate functions, even inside one organisation. Energy resources generates

and extracts: power, fuel, mining and forestry, drawing raw supply from the world for direct use as fuel or material. Sanitation decides whether that activity is properly contained and handled, holding it within thresholds of cleanliness, containment and waste handling. Preservational keeps the account of ecological conditions and consequences. A sanitation permit does not make an outcome acceptable. Only the

preservational account shows whether it was. Medical agents treat, care and intervene, including in health and fitness. The scientific body approves or denies against established evidence, and a result counts for it only when it holds up to testing and repetition. Nutritional provision records and reviews production, preparation, availability, consumption, diets and requirements: the whole supply of what people take

in. Pharmaceuticals divide along the same line: prescribing and administering a drug is medical, and accounting for what people are supplied to consume is nutritional provision. Cooking a meal is an action. Accounting for whether people are actually nourished is the authoritative function. Manufacturing produces goods to authorised commercial requirements; read widely, it is the whole system of supply and

demand that turns orders into things. Commercial approves or denies proposals, commissions, contracts, loans and commitments. Financial records and reviews accounts, capital, transactions, investments and obligations: the ledgers of banks, the positions of investors and brokers, the assessments of tax authorities. Funding and energy both sustain an operation, yet they remain separate categories: one is an economic relationship and the

other is physical capacity. Content creation produces expressive work: performance, entertainment and commentary. Media distribution decides access, hosting, deployment, publication and circulation. Its occupants are platform founders and administrators, network programmers and the people who deploy servers and set the rules of reach. Its approval takes three forms: permission to publish, the decision to recommend or share, and the audience’s

acceptance of the claim. News journalism investigates and returns the account. Publishing words or video does not make anyone a journalist. Purpose decides the position. Cultural agents carry family, community and everyday social life through homes, workplaces and gatherings. The educational body approves or denies guidance and qualifications against knowledge and competence requirements. It certifies competence; it does not license

cultural life. The religious position records and reviews conduct against spiritual and ethical frameworks, ethical frameworks without religion included. It asks whether inherited interpretations still hold, whether practice matched professed principle, and what now needs reconsidering. Acting, permitting and accounting are different questions. No answer to one settles the others. Functional distinction locates responsibility. A failed proposal, a bad approval,

poor execution and a misleading account are different failures with different remedies. The account must reach someone who can respond. A record that informs no later request, decision, correction or challenge has no corrective power. Every domain depends on conditions it cannot produce alone. Public authority needs implementation. Implementation needs infrastructure, resources and people. People need health, nourishment, goods and

economic coordination. Accountability needs communication and social life. Connections between domains provide recourse. When one route closes, an account can travel through another body and return as pressure on the first. Together these make the model more than a list of professions. It is a classification of capabilities joined to an account of how social activity is sustained and made

answerable. The nine bodies run in a fixed order, and body 9 returns to body 1. Governmental activity gives public authority its basis and its accountability. Legal activity gives claims and disputes an organised means of submission and response. Enforcement connects authorised decisions to interventions in the world. Development logistics supplies the planning, infrastructure, maintenance and movement those interventions require.

Energy resources supplies material and energetic capacity. Medical care and nutritional provision sustain the people doing the work. Manufacturing, commercial and financial functions handle goods, commitments and the accounting of resources. Creation, distribution and journalism handle expression, circulation and the public account. Cultural life is where people meet, contest and live with all of it. Their shared conviction becomes public

expectation, and that expectation becomes government’s agenda. The order descends through authority, access and resources. It runs from the body that frames public power, through the bodies that give that power legal form and practical force, then the material, human, economic and expressive conditions that sustain it, and finally into the everyday life that answers back. The loop is the

route authority travels and the route an account travels back. Dependencies run wider than the loop. Development depends on finance, medicine on logistics, and journalism on people, equipment and power. The loop traces how authority flows, and the network of dependencies shows what each body needs to function. A dependency names a need. It does not certify that the need

is met. Funding and energy show why the categories stay apart even where they combine: both drive an operation the way fuel and finance both keep an engine running, and each still has its own triad to account for it. The loop also reads in reverse, as evidence. When a story refused by its distributor reaches the public through cultural

life and returns as pressure, the link that carried it is alive, and a live link testifies to the bodies behind it. Government is still holding itself to the transparent release of truth. Law is still carrying claims and representing rights in court. Enforcement still has agents in the field gathering what is real and deciding on it. Those agents

can still move and plan their operations, and energy still powers them. Each live link is evidence that the bodies it depends on are working. Each broken link shows where to look. Body 9 closes the loop. A community’s religious and ethical life is the democratic expression of convictions that are hard to consolidate into single claims: what is owed

to a neighbour, what honesty requires, what a shared resource means. Those convictions shape what the population expects. The population makes government representative on its behalf, and through participation, advocacy, voting and representation its expectations become government’s agenda, where they are argued into claims and the constitution is tested, rectified or amended in response. The loop carries shared conviction into

explicit proposals, carries proposals into practical consequences, and carries those consequences back into public understanding. What moves through it is an interpretation of reality, not reality itself. “Law” in this model has three registers. All three enter the same argument: in the water case, test results constrain the claims about the water, ethical commitments define responsibility, and public law sets

the remedy. They remain distinct. A ruling settles a question within its authority, but it does not settle physical fact. Religious review has to be able to question its own past readings in the light of evidence, or the review becomes a defence and stops being an account. A review resolves in one of two ways, and the model keeps

them apart. A new interpretation forms inside a tradition: religious review reaffirms an earlier reading, exposes a tension, or reinterprets what a duty requires now, and the tradition’s own process decides whether that reading becomes canonical. A new law forms in the state: the account goes forward as a request, governmental agents draft and submit, and the constitutional body approves

or denies. Where a law already exists, legal agents argue it and the judicial body rules. In the water case both happen. The tradition comes to hold that care for a neighbour includes honesty about shared dangers, and the public asks for a law requiring water-test results to be published. Religious review motivates and informs the legal change; it never

enacts it. When review reaffirms an old reading, it explains why the new evidence leaves its commitments intact. When it finds tension, it says so, and returns that tension to community life to be lived with, argued over and changed. Whatever law results stays open to review, and the loop continues. Residents who share a water supply suspect contamination. The

concern forms in bodies 8 and 9 before it ever reaches government. A resident films the discoloured water and posts the video: content creation, enact and submit. Media distribution lets it circulate, and viewers accept or reject what it claims. Both are approvals, and rejection still spreads it. News journalism checks the claim, tests the evidence and returns a fuller

account. That account enters cultural life through families, workplaces and neighbours, and a community meeting is called to request precautions. At the meeting the educational body endorses or rejects the proposed guidance, and an informed speaker explains the grounds. Religious review then asks how the response fits the community’s inherited understanding of reality, truth and its obligations to others. It

returns a shared conviction: protecting our neighbours requires honest disclosure of dangers to the water we share. Through participation, advocacy, voting and representation, that conviction becomes a demand on government. Religious communities contribute alongside every other community and citizen. The conviction carries weight because it is shared, not because one community speaks for everyone. The same person moves through several

positions in that sequence. The resident who filmed the water is a creator, then an audience member, then a voice at the meeting. The positions do not merge because she occupies them in turn. From there the concern travels the whole loop. A journalist holds an evidenced account of an over-budget bridge contract, and the main media distributor refuses to

carry it. The model shows where the account goes next. A refusal in one domain does not end the matter while another legitimate route remains. That is the purpose of the descending order: a distributor holds a veto over its own channel and over nothing else. Two conditions govern that route: The route must be genuinely open. If the same

gatekeeper controls the alternative channel, renaming it creates no new path. Circulation is not truth. A true account and a false rumour can both spread. The route guarantees a hearing, and evidence and review decide the claim. The structure is set. The next stage of construction fills in the operating rules: A cannot-do list for every triad, built to the

standard auditorial already meets. Who occupies each authorising position, and where its remit ends. What makes an account independent enough and open enough to challenge. How conflicting approvals or accounts are resolved. Further routes of recourse, beyond the journalism-to-culture route. Cases that cross several domains at once.
